Check the asset and component boundary against the room

The baseline introduced at the start is a real scene with object records. The room was constructed and rendered in the local Unreal 5.8.1 editor on an RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell using DX12 and Shader Model 6. Its seven visible cube components provide a measured version of the sharing and registration contracts derived above. They are the same baseline used in the independent Lumen essay; the additional fixtures later in this article live in a separate extension map so these controlled comparisons stay reproducible.

Seven visible static-mesh components make the room: five enclosing surfaces and two blocks. All seven refer to /Engine/BasicShapes/Cube.Cube. There are seven placements and one shared mesh asset, with five material assets. The floor, ceiling, and back wall share the neutral material. The relationship is measurable: duplicating a box-shaped object does not duplicate the cube's vertex buffer. Each component supplies its own placement and material binding.

The authoring script creates a StaticMeshActor, obtains its StaticMeshComponent, calls set_static_mesh with the shared cube, assigns a material, and sets the actor's scale and location. These are public authoring operations. When the component registers, the render-state lifecycle described in the previous chapter creates the rendering representation. The script does not directly allocate an FPrimitiveSceneInfo, write GPU Scene buffers, or create draw commands; registration and renderer processing do that.

The matte block's cube begins at local coordinates bounded by ±50 cm. With no rotation, its world transform is \(p_w=t+Sp_l\), where \(t=(77.5,285,52.5)\) cm and \(S=\operatorname{diag}(0.95,1.1,1.05)\). The local corner \((50,50,50)\) becomes \((125,340,105)\) cm, and the opposite corner becomes \((30,230,0)\). Those values explain its actual bounds and floor contact. The shared cube asset never changes to contain those world coordinates.

For a general rotation and scale matrix \(A\), a local axis-aligned box with center \(c\) and half-extents \(e\) transforms to a conservative world axis-aligned box with center \(Ac+t\) and half-extents \(|A|e\), where absolute value is elementwise. Write every local point as \(c+\delta\) with \(|\delta_j|\le e_j\). Then each world-axis deviation obeys

\[|(A\delta)_i|=\left|\sum_j A_{ij}\delta_j\right|\le\sum_j|A_{ij}|e_j.\]

For each axis, the bound is attained at a box corner, so this is the exact AABB of the transformed box. It remains only a bound on the mesh, which matters for rotated thin objects and for deformation that extends past the asset's original box.

Inspect the room as geometry and view-dependent work

The lit image hides the simplicity of the source geometry and the sequence of decisions that makes it visible. Switch the same camera to wireframe. The walls and blocks are transformed instances of a cube; they do not become complex geometry because indirect light produces gradients across their surfaces. A material, a lighting cache, or a depth discontinuity can create image detail without adding vertices.

The next view displays bounds. The renderer can reject a component's bound without visiting every triangle. A bound that intersects the view gives permission to do more work; it does not prove that all the component's triangles are visible. The matte block can hide part of the back wall even while both component bounds lie inside the camera, and a shadow view has different visibility from this camera. The bounds overlay shows the culling input. It does not show every internal culling decision or measure draw count.

The chain runs from component to proxy behavior to scene membership to GPU-addressable placement to view work. The component owns editable state. The proxy presents a rendering snapshot and geometry-specific behavior. Scene info associates that proxy with the renderer's persistent organization. GPU Scene makes the needed data accessible to shaders, and the view chooses the relevant work. None of these transitions copies the seven UStaticMeshComponent objects byte-for-byte into a GPU buffer.

A depth image is the next check because it contains the result of visible-surface selection without the full lighting solution. The front of the matte block wins where it overlaps the back wall; the back wall remains represented elsewhere. The stored depth convention and the debug view's display mapping are separate matters. For this capture, a native post-process material reads linear SceneDepth in centimeters and divides by 1,000 after tone mapping. Its shader output reaches white at ten meters. This display is a custom diagnostic of the actual depth buffer, not raw device Z, and it is not the engine's default depth-view scale.

Arda's corresponding debugging feature should expose both levels: human-readable overlays and machine-readable records with units. A bounding-box screenshot answers whether placement is plausible; a bounds export can verify exact values. A depth visualization reveals discontinuities, and an untonemapped depth export supports numerical comparison. Keeping the forms together makes the scene system testable without confusing a display transform with the underlying data.

Separate the room's surfaces from the light they reflect

The baseline's seven components produce several per-pixel descriptions. Base color comes first: it answers what material property the winning surface supplies, before indirect illumination makes the neutral floor appear tinted. The red wall's material and the red light reflected onto the floor are different quantities. The floor keeps its neutral base color even where its final lit appearance becomes red, which is why deferred rendering separates surface evaluation from lighting evaluation.

The world-normal view answers a different question: which way does the visible surface face? Identical materials on the floor and back wall do not imply identical illumination, because their normals differ. Under nonuniform scale, transform normals with the inverse transpose of the linear transform and normalize them. The rule follows from perpendicularity. For a tangent \(v\), \(n^Tv=0\). After transforming the tangent to \(Av\), choose \(n'=A^{-T}n\), giving \((n')^TAv=n^Tv=0\). Multiplying a normal by \(A\) as if it were a position breaks that relation under general nonuniform scale.

The matte block uses roughness 0.7 and metallic 0; the glossy block uses roughness 0.12 and metallic 0.85. Roughness changes the distribution of reflection directions, and metallic changes how base color and reflectance participate in the shading model. These values are shader inputs, not labels such as “reflective object” stored on the actor. A low-roughness surface needs sharper directional information from the reflection system than a rough diffuse surface does.

These views select shading inputs; they do not exhaust the engine's packed GBuffer layout, which can depend on material features and renderer settings. The contract survives those implementation choices: visible-surface evaluation makes material properties available, and lighting combines them with positions, normals, light data, shadows, and indirect-light estimates. The light component is not another material slot on each wall. It contributes illumination to relevant receivers through renderer light data and lighting passes.

The ceiling light is a movable RectLightComponent with 2,400 lumens, source width 130 cm, source height 120 cm, and attenuation radius 2,000 cm. It sits at (0,270,298) cm and points downward. Exposure stays fixed for the capture sequence. These settings make the experiment reproducible, but the image is not a radiometric comparison with the CPU lab: the reduced renderer uses its own normalized radiance units, while Unreal applies its light-unit conventions, shading, reconstruction, exposure, and display pipeline.

Change one room and follow the representations that must respond

Move the matte block while keeping its mesh and material. The capture script sets its mobility to movable and translates it by (−100,−80,0) cm, so its center moves from (77.5,285,52.5) to (−22.5,205,52.5). There is still one shared cube asset and the same material binding. Placement, bounds, visibility, shadows, tracing instances, and affected lighting history all have to respond. The update message “the mesh changed” is therefore incomplete.

Next change only the light's intensity and color, leaving the moved block in place. The light becomes 7,200 lumens with linear RGB (1,0.55,0.25). The cube geometry and material base colors stay the same. Direct lighting changes as soon as the new light data is consumed; indirect illumination and histories follow their own update policies. A light change does not by itself require a material recapture; cached illumination is what changes.

The original room's tracing and caching views show two more representations. Lumen's mesh distance fields describe geometric proximity for software tracing. Its cards parameterize surfaces for material and lighting storage. The two answer different questions about the same seven components: a card is not a triangle acceleration structure, and a distance field is not a material texture. A valid component transform must place both consistently, but their resolution, update mechanism, and failure modes differ.

The baseline and its extensions cover each stage of the architecture. Authoring objects provide semantic identity and editable values. Proxies and scene info organize renderer-owned behavior and membership. GPU records provide compact placement and material access. View passes resolve surfaces, deferred shading consumes their properties, and Lumen keeps additional geometry and radiance representations so light can arrive from surfaces beyond the camera's first hit. The independent Lumen walkthrough follows those caches numerically and visually. A scene architecture therefore needs each consumer to keep its own representation while tracking which object and revision it describes.

The reproducibility bundle contains the room creation script, a small project-local editor bridge for verified viewport modes, engine configuration, the exported scene inventory, capture commands, and image hashes. Internal proxy addresses, primitive packed indices, and draw counts were not instrumented here, so the essay does not infer them from actor labels. A future Arda implementation should expose those identities explicitly, with generations and revision numbers, which would let a trace follow this block from authoring edit to upload, draw, tracing update, and lighting invalidation without relying on a screenshot to guess the transition.
