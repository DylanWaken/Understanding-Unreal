One room is enough to expose the problem: light leaves a ceiling emitter, scatters from colored walls and objects, and reaches a camera. A path tracer estimates those paths directly. Lumen spends a bounded amount of work on a set of representations that share and reuse lighting. This essay derives their common transport problem, identifies exactly what the real-time method approximates, and follows the actual engine data through a frame.

The argument has three parts: the path integral and a working renderer; the mathematical approximation and its cost; and deferred shading, shadows, and the complete Lumen pipeline. The scene comparisons are actual Unreal renders. The smaller CPU reference is separately identified. Numerical resource inspection complements the images: a colored atlas alone cannot expose an address, PDF, history weight, or invalid bit.

This chapter first derives a surface path tracer, then uses that derivation to describe what a cached real-time renderer changes. The mathematical reference is stationary, unpolarized geometric optics in vacuum, with opaque surfaces unless an extension is explicitly introduced. These assumptions describe the reference problem; they do not describe every feature of Unreal Engine.

The path notation follows the light-to-sensor convention of Eric Veach's thesis. In particular, the arrow in the three-point notation follows light, even in a sensor-response expression: \(W_e^{(j)}(x\to x')\) is importance emitted from the sensor at \(x'\) toward \(x\). Veach defines this explicitly in §8.1, printed pp. 220–222. The derivations and numerical examples below are original explanations using that convention. Veach, full thesis

Part I · Fix the quantities before tracing a ray

Let \(\mathcal M\) be the union of all participating surface domains, including emitters and the sensor. An emitter can also scatter light; it is not removed from \(\mathcal M\). The emitted-radiance function \(L_e\) is zero where there is no emission, and the sensor response \(W_e^{(j)}\) is zero outside the sensor domain for measurement \(j\).

Symbol Meaning and convention
\(x,y\in\mathcal M\) Surface positions; distances use metres in the derivation.
\(\omega_i,\omega_o\) Unit directions pointing away from a scattering point, toward the incident source and outgoing receiver respectively.
\(\mathbf n_g,\mathbf n_s\) Geometric and shading normals. The reference example initially uses \(\mathbf n_g=\mathbf n_s\).
\(dA,d\sigma,d\sigma_x^\perp\) Surface area, solid angle, and projected solid angle; \(d\sigma_x^\perp=|\mathbf n_g(x)\cdot\omega|d\sigma\).
\(L_e,L_i,L_o\) Emitted, incident, and outgoing radiance. \(L(x\to y)\) is outgoing radiance at \(x\), toward \(y\).
\(f_s(x;\omega_i\to\omega_o)\) BSDF expressed relative to the stated projected-solid-angle measure.
\(V(x\leftrightarrow y)\) Visibility of the open segment, 0 or 1 in the opaque reference.
\(G(x\leftrightarrow y)\) Visibility times the two geometric cosines divided by squared distance.
\(\bar x=(x_0,\ldots,x_k)\) A path with \(k\) edges, ordered from emitter \(x_0\) to sensor \(x_k\).
\(\Omega_k,\Omega,\mu_k,\mu\) Length-specific path space, their disjoint union, and area-product measures.
\(I_j,f_j,W_e^{(j)}\) Sensor measurement, path contribution function, and sensor response.
\(p_A,p_\sigma\) Densities per unit area and per unit solid angle. A discrete light-selection probability is written \(P_\ell\).
\(\beta\) Running camera-path weight; not a radiance buffer.

Radiant energy \(Q\) has units joules, and radiant flux is \(\Phi=dQ/dt\), in watts. Irradiance is incoming flux per surface area, \(E=d\Phi/dA\). Radiance is the density of flux in projected area and solid angle:

\[ d\Phi=L(x,\omega)|\mathbf n_g(x)\cdot\omega|\,dA(x)\,d\sigma(\omega). \tag{M01} \]

Thus radiance has units \(\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}\); irradiance has units \(\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\). An irradiance buffer and a radiance buffer cannot be interchanged merely because each has three floating-point channels.

A diffuse emitter with constant radiance \(10\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}\) and emitting area \(0.02\ \mathrm{m^2}\) sends total power

\[ \Phi=A L\int_{H^2}\cos\theta\,d\sigma=A L\pi=0.628318531\ \mathrm W. \tag{M02} \]

For a cone of solid angle \(\Delta\sigma\) around direction \(\omega_0\), replacing every cosine by \(c_0=\mathbf n_g\cdot\omega_0\) gives the small-cone approximation \(\Phi\approx A L c_0\Delta\sigma\). With \(c_0=0.8\) and \(\Delta\sigma=0.5\ \mathrm{sr}\), that approximation is \(0.08\ \mathrm W\), or 80 mW. For a circular cone wholly above the emitting horizon, its exact cosine integral is \(c_0\pi\sin^2\alpha\), where \(\Delta\sigma=2\pi(1-\cos\alpha)\). The exact answer is consequently

\[ \Phi=A L c_0\Delta\sigma\left(1-\frac{\Delta\sigma}{4\pi}\right) =0.076816901\ \mathrm W. \tag{M03} \]

This example distinguishes a differential relation from a finite-region approximation rather than treating a representative cosine as an exact integral.

Derive the local rendering equation

The BSDF converts irradiance arriving from a differential range of directions into outgoing radiance:

\[ dL_o(x,\omega_o)=f_s(x;\omega_i\to\omega_o)L_i(x,\omega_i) |\mathbf n_g(x)\cdot\omega_i|\,d\sigma(\omega_i). \tag{M04} \]

Its units are inverse steradians. Integration, plus local emission, gives

\[ L_o(x,\omega_o)=L_e(x,\omega_o)+ \int_{S^2}f_s(x;\omega_i\to\omega_o)L_i(x,\omega_i)\,d\sigma_x^\perp(\omega_i). \tag{M05} \]

For an opaque one-sided reflector, \(f_s\) is zero on unsupported direction pairs, so the integral reduces to the incident hemisphere. The sphere notation also accommodates transmission when its BSDF and medium conventions have been specified. Absolute cosines by themselves do not make a one-sided material two-sided.

In vacuum, radiance remains constant along an unobstructed ray. If \(y\) is the first intersection from \(x\) in direction \(\omega_i\), then

\[ L_i(x,\omega_i)=L_o(y,-\omega_i). \tag{M06} \]

There is no inverse-square factor in (M06). Inverse-square dilution appears when a finite source area is converted into the solid angle it occupies, not when evaluating radiance along one ray. A miss returns the environment radiance. For the surface-only derivation, an environment can be represented by an enclosing emitting boundary and a limiting construction; practical code uses a separate directional endpoint.

For a Lambertian reflector with reflectance \(\rho\), \(f_s=\rho/\pi\). Under a prescribed constant incident radiance \(L_0\),

\[ L_o-L_e=\frac{\rho}{\pi}\int_{H^2}L_0\cos\theta\,d\sigma=\rho L_0. \tag{M07} \]

For \(\rho=0.8\) and \(L_0=2\), the reflected radiance is exactly \(1.6\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}\). This is a local constant-environment check. It does not assert that an arbitrary enclosed interreflection scene has the same incident field.

The normal used by the material is not the geometric normal

Surface area, visibility, and the solid-angle Jacobian depend on \(\mathbf n_g\). A normal map changes the angular scattering model. Suppose a renderer wants a reciprocal local shading-frame function \(b_s\) multiplied by \(|\mathbf n_s\cdot\omega_i|\). It can express that convention using the geometric measure in (M05) by defining an effective radiance BSDF

\[ f_s^{\rm eff}(\omega_i\to\omega_o)= b_s(\omega_i\to\omega_o) \frac{|\mathbf n_s\cdot\omega_i|}{|\mathbf n_g\cdot\omega_i|}, \tag{M08} \]

on valid direction pairs with nonzero denominator. The product \(f_s^{\rm eff}|\mathbf n_g\cdot\omega_i|\) then reproduces the intended shading cosine. This is an algebraic description of a shading-normal convention, not a proof that the resulting model conserves energy. Geometric-hemisphere tests and grazing-angle handling still matter; normal mapping can break reciprocity and energy conservation.

A camera path solves outgoing radiance toward the camera. At a surface with known camera-side direction \(\omega_o\), it samples a new light-side direction \(\omega_i\) and evaluates \(f_s(\omega_i\to\omega_o)\). Sampling the path in reverse order does not authorize transposing this BSDF. Adjoint operators are needed when transporting importance through nonsymmetric scattering, as in the complementary light-tracing formulation. One must state the transported quantity and measure before applying a correction; the blanket instruction “camera tracing uses the adjoint BSDF” is unsafe. Veach's discussion of shading normals and adjoint transport is in chapters 4–5. Veach, chapters 4–5

Convert directions into surface points

Let \(\omega_{xy}=(y-x)/r\), with \(r=\|y-x\|\). A small surface patch at \(y\) subtends solid angle at \(x\)

\[ d\sigma_x(\omega_{xy})= \frac{|\mathbf n_g(y)\cdot(-\omega_{xy})|}{r^2}\,dA(y). \tag{M09} \]

The cosine is at the target whose area is being projected. Multiplying by the cosine at the receiving surface and restricting to visible first intersections yields

\[ G(x\leftrightarrow y)=V(x\leftrightarrow y) \frac{|\mathbf n_g(x)\cdot\omega_{xy}|\, |\mathbf n_g(y)\cdot(-\omega_{xy})|}{r^2}. \tag{M10} \]

For visible surface elements, \(d\sigma_x^\perp=G(x\leftrightarrow y)dA(y)\). Extending the integration over all scene surfaces requires the visibility factor to avoid counting surfaces behind the first intersection.

What to inspect in the real pair. The two actual Unreal renders directly below are the same matched pair discussed again in §08. They are convergence and path comparisons, not dumps of sample directions. They can show the integral's visible result—soft indirect gradients and finite-sample structure—but not the schematic's individual cone arrows. The replay data later in the essay is what supplies exact cache quantities; this diagram only fixes the measure before those quantities appear.

Writing the BSDF at \(x\) as \(f_s(y\to x\to z)\) gives the three-point equation

\[ L(x\to z)=L_e(x\to z)+ \int_{\mathcal M}L(y\to x)f_s(y\to x\to z)G(y\leftrightarrow x)\,dA(y). \tag{M11} \]

The sensor measurement is

\[ I_j=\int_{\mathcal M\times\mathcal M} W_e^{(j)}(x\to y)L(x\to y)G(x\leftrightarrow y)\,dA(x)dA(y). \tag{M12} \]

Although the arrow is \(x\to y\), sensor importance in this expression originates at \(y\). The arrow continues to identify physical light flow.

A patch calculation. Two tiny mutually visible patches, each \(10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2}\), face one another at distance 2 m. To the differential-patch approximation, \(G=1/4\ \mathrm{m^{-2}}\). If the first emits radiance \(L=5\), the power received by the second is

\[ d\Phi=L G\,dA_1dA_2=1.25\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm W. \tag{M13} \]

There is no BSDF in this incident-power calculation. The incident irradiance is \(L G dA_1=1.25\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\). If the receiving BSDF toward a selected outgoing direction is \(0.2\ \mathrm{sr^{-1}}\), the outgoing radiance due to this source patch is \(2.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}\). A further outgoing direction or receiver must be integrated before that radiance becomes outgoing power.

Expand transport into a path integral

Define the transport operator \(T\) as the integral on the right of (M11), excluding emission. Then

\[ L=L_e+TL. \tag{M14} \]

Repeated substitution gives

\[ L=L_e+TL_e+T^2L_e+\cdots. \tag{M15} \]

Each application of \(T\) adds one scattering event. A sufficient convergence condition is \(\|T\|<1\) in a suitable norm. Absorption and escape can provide it; an enclosed, perfectly reflecting system with continuously injected energy need not have a finite steady solution. Convergence assumptions are part of the model, not a consequence of writing an infinite sum.

Insert (M15) into (M12). For \(\bar x=(x_0,\ldots,x_k)\), set

\[ \begin{aligned} f_j(\bar x)={}&L_e(x_0\to x_1) \left[\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}G(x_i\leftrightarrow x_{i+1})\right]\\ &\times\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}f_s(x_{i-1}\to x_i\to x_{i+1})\right] W_e^{(j)}(x_{k-1}\to x_k),\\ d\mu_k(\bar x)={}&dA(x_0)\cdots dA(x_k). \end{aligned} \tag{M16} \]

The empty BSDF product for \(k=1\) is 1. That term is emission seen directly by the sensor. \(k=2\) has one scattering event, and \(k=3\) has two. The sensor is not counted as a material-scattering event here.

Let \(\Omega\) be the disjoint union of the \(\Omega_k\), and define \(\mu(D)=\sum_k\mu_k(D\cap\Omega_k)\). The final expression is

\[ I_j=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega_k}f_j(\bar x)\,d\mu_k(\bar x) =\int_\Omega f_j(\bar x)\,d\mu(\bar x). \tag{M17} \]

This is a reorganization of the rendering equation, not a new physical model. It exposes every factor that a renderer can approximate: emission, geometry and visibility, scattering, and sensor response. The ordering and contribution-function construction correspond to Veach §8.2. Veach, chapter 8

The dimensions also provide a check. Ignoring the dimensionless SI angle unit, \(L_e\) contributes \(\mathrm{W/m^2}\), the \(k\) geometric factors contribute \(\mathrm{m^{-2k}}\), and the area measure contributes \(\mathrm{m^{2(k+1)}}\); their product is watts. \(W_e^{(j)}\) converts that power to the desired measurement unit. Its units are a chosen sensor convention, not universally “inverse watts.” If steradians are retained as bookkeeping labels, each angular integration supplies the angle factor that its BSDF density requires.

Singular paths require their own measures

The ordinary area-product expression is simplest for finite-area endpoints and nonsingular scattering. A pinhole camera, point light, or ideal mirror introduces a delta constraint. A delta is not an enormous finite BSDF or a tiny area with an arbitrary PDF. It is a different measure component, with probability mass or a constrained sampling map.

A camera tracer can sample a glossy or diffuse receiver, follow a specular reflection, and hit a finite-area emitter with nonzero probability. Therefore “camera path tracing cannot render caustics” is too broad. A configuration requiring a continuously sampled direction to hit a mathematical point emitter after an exact specular constraint can instead have zero probability under that sampler. The practical test is support: can this sampler generate the contributing family under its appropriate measure? Specialized connections, bidirectional methods, or manifold methods can expand support; more samples cannot repair exactly zero support.

Monte Carlo estimates and the camera-path weight

For a path density \(p\) with respect to \(\mu\), the elementary estimator is

\[ \widehat I_j=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\frac{f_j(\bar X_n)}{p(\bar X_n)},\qquad \bar X_n\sim p. \tag{M18} \]

Its expectation is \(I_j\) because integrating \(f_j/p\) against \(p\,d\mu\) cancels the sampling density. This requires integrability and sampling support wherever the contribution is nonzero, up to measure-zero sets. Finite variance is an additional requirement for the usual variance-over-\(N\) error formula. Independence is convenient for that variance formula; unbiasedness follows from the expectation of each correctly weighted sample.

If complete paths are sampled by first choosing length \(k\), the length PMF is part of \(p\). A practical path tracer instead reuses one random walk to estimate contributions at several lengths. Linearity of expectation permits this reuse; those contributions need not be independent.

At a surface \(x\), fix the outgoing direction \(\omega_o\) toward the already constructed camera subpath. Sample the incident direction from a solid-angle density \(p_\sigma(\omega_i\mid x,\omega_o)\). Equation (M05) yields the throughput update

\[ \beta_{\rm next}=\beta\, \frac{f_s(x;\omega_i\to\omega_o)|\mathbf n_g(x)\cdot\omega_i|} {p_\sigma(\omega_i\mid x,\omega_o)}. \tag{M19} \]

The new ray finds the next vertex, thereby sampling visibility through first intersection. To compare this construction with the area-product path density, the corresponding next-vertex density is

\[ p_A(y\mid x)=p_\sigma(\omega_{xy}\mid x)\, \frac{|\mathbf n_g(y)\cdot(-\omega_{xy})|}{\|y-x\|^2}. \tag{M20} \]

In \(f_j/p\), the target cosine and inverse-square factor in \(G\) cancel those in (M20), leaving precisely the receiving cosine in (M19). This cancellation explains why an ordinary ray-path loop does not explicitly multiply \(1/r^2\) at every bounce.

Cosine sampling is obtained from \(u,v\) uniform on \([0,1)^2\):

\[ \omega_i=\left(\sqrt u\cos(2\pi v),\sqrt u\sin(2\pi v),\sqrt{1-u}\right), \qquad p_\sigma(\omega_i)=\frac{\cos\theta}{\pi}. \tag{M21} \]

Rotate this local direction into the surface frame. For Lambertian scattering with matching normals, (M19) simplifies to \(\beta_{\rm next}=\beta\rho\). In the constant-environment example, every sample returns \(0.8\times2=1.6\); the local directional estimator has zero variance. A visible emitter occupying a small part of the hemisphere is a different integrand and will not have zero variance.

What does a unit-weight pinhole ray measure?

A physical sensor integrates optical power over aperture, pixel area, wavelength, and time. Its weighting belongs in \(W_e^{(j)}\). A common teaching renderer deliberately uses a simpler normalized measurement:

\[ \overline I_j=\frac1{|P_j|}\int_{P_j}L_{\rm ray}(u,v)\,du\,dv, \tag{M22} \]

where \(P_j\) is the pixel's film-coordinate footprint and \(L_{\rm ray}\) is radiance arriving along its pinhole ray. Uniform subpixel sampling then permits \(\beta=1\). The camera position is conditioned on; it is not assigned a finite ordinary area PDF. This specialization avoids dividing by a fictitious “pinhole area.”

For a vertical field of view \(\theta_v\), aspect \(a=W/H\), camera forward \(\mathbf f\), right \(\mathbf r\), and up \(\mathbf u\), a jittered pixel \((i,j)\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} s_x&=\left(2\frac{i+\xi_x}{W}-1\right)a\tan(\theta_v/2),\\ s_y&=\left(1-2\frac{j+\xi_y}{H}\right)\tan(\theta_v/2),\\ \omega&=\operatorname{normalize}(\mathbf f+s_x\mathbf r+s_y\mathbf u). \end{aligned} \tag{M23} \]

Each pixel must include its own \(i,j\); sampling \(\xi_x,\xi_y\) over the entire image for every pixel produces an image of the scene's average rather than a camera image. In a right-handed convention, \(\mathbf r=\operatorname{normalize}(\mathbf f\times\mathbf u_{world})\) and \(\mathbf u=\mathbf r\times\mathbf f\).

To see why (M22) is not automatically physical sensor irradiance, consider a small planar aperture of area \(A_l\), parallel to a film at axial distance \(f\). At film angle \(\theta\), its solid angle is approximately \(A_l\cos\theta/r^2\). Multiplying by the film receiving cosine and using \(r=f/\cos\theta\) gives

\[ E_{film}\simeq L\frac{A_l}{f^2}\cos^4\theta. \tag{M24} \]

This is the small-aperture geometric construction, before lens transmission and other optical effects. With \(L=10\), aperture radius 5 mm, \(f=50\) mm, and \(\theta=0\), \(E_{film}=0.314159265\ \mathrm{W/m^2}\). A 5 µm square pixel receives \(7.85398163\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm W\). Focal length and pixel pitch alone cannot determine this power: aperture area is essential. The normalized radiance image remains useful, but its measurement definition must be stated.

Direct light sampling, MIS, and termination

Waiting for a randomly continued path to hit a small emitter is inefficient. Next-event estimation (NEE) samples that final connection explicitly. At \(x\), choose light \(\ell\) with probability \(P_\ell(x)\), then sample emitter point \(y\) with conditional density \(p_A(y\mid\ell,x)\). The combined area density is \(P_\ell p_A\). In solid angle at \(x\),

\[ p_{L,\sigma}(\omega_{xy})=P_\ell(x)p_A(y\mid\ell,x) \frac{r^2}{|\mathbf n_g(y)\cdot(-\omega_{xy})|}. \tag{M25} \]

With \(c_x=|\mathbf n_g(x)\cdot\omega_{xy}|\), the unweighted light-sampled contribution is

\[ C_L=\beta\, \frac{f_s(x;\omega_{xy}\to\omega_o)L_e(y\to x)V(x\leftrightarrow y)c_x} {p_{L,\sigma}(\omega_{xy})}. \tag{M26} \]

Equivalently, use \(\beta f_s L_e G/(P_\ell p_A)\) in area measure. Mixing the denominator of one form with the numerator of the other is an error.

A numerical NEE sample. There is one uniformly sampled emitter of area \(0.25\ \mathrm{m^2}\), so \(P_\ell=1\), \(p_A=4\ \mathrm{m^{-2}}\). A selected sample is 2 m directly above the receiving point with both cosines 1. Then \(p_{L,\sigma}=16\ \mathrm{sr^{-1}}\). For \(\rho=0.8\), \(L_e=10\), visibility 1, and \(\beta=1\),

\[ C_L=\frac{(0.8/\pi)10}{16}=\frac1{2\pi}=0.159154943. \tag{M27} \]

This is one sample's contribution, not the exact integral over the finite emitter. Other sampled points have different distance and cosines. If this same light is selected with probability \(1/2\) among two lights, its selected-sample value doubles; it is selected half as often. Omitting \(P_\ell\) changes the expected result.

The BSDF continuation can also hit this emitter. Adding both complete direct-light estimates doubles the same path family. Multiple importance sampling combines them. For one light sample and one BSDF sample, a useful choice is the power heuristic

\[ w_L(\omega)=\frac{p_L(\omega)^2}{p_L(\omega)^2+p_B(\omega)^2}, \qquad w_B(\omega)=\frac{p_B(\omega)^2}{p_L(\omega)^2+p_B(\omega)^2}. \tag{M28} \]

Both PDFs must use the same measure and the same receiver context. The emitter-hit PDF includes the probability of selecting that emitter from the previous scattering point. With \(n_L,n_B\) samples, replace each \(p\) by \(np\) inside the weights and average each technique's samples by its own sample count.

Why does this work? For the direct integrand \(F\),

\[ \mathbb E\!\left[w_L(X_L)\frac{F(X_L)}{p_L(X_L)}+ w_B(X_B)\frac{F(X_B)}{p_B(X_B)}\right] =\int(w_L+w_B)F\,d\sigma=\int F\,d\sigma. \tag{M29} \]

This proof is the important part: the weights partition the contribution. Where a technique has zero support its weight must be zero, and where only one technique can generate the path its weight is one. MIS is described generally in Veach chapter 9; the exponent-two choice here is one instance, not the only valid combination. Veach, chapter 9

Real numerical anchor. M27's selected NEE sample is 1/(2π) = 0.159154943 for the stated emitter and geometry; M28 defines the two power-heuristic weights. The CPU reference in §08 exercises direct-light MIS, but its published image is a path/convergence comparison, not a visualization of these arrows. The later GPU replay is what turns cache terms into byte-level observations.

Delta events and mixtures

An ideal mirror selects one deterministic reflected direction. Its discrete probability mass cannot be compared numerically with a density per steradian. A BSDF sample interface should return a transport weight, the applicable PDF or mass, and whether the sampled event is delta. A mirror event contributes its reflection factor divided by its branch-selection probability; its distributional BSDF must not be inserted as a finite number into (M19).

For a diffuse-plus-glossy continuous mixture, evaluate the full continuous BSDF and its actual mixture density \(p_B=\sum_b P_b p_b\). If a delta branch is selected, use the corresponding discrete contribution and branch PMF. A mixed material can still have a continuous part worth sampling by NEE; skip NEE only when there is no connectable continuous component. An emitter reached through a previous delta event receives weight one against ordinary continuous emitter sampling. A very narrow but finite glossy lobe is not automatically a delta.

Russian roulette removes work without deleting the tail in expectation

After local contributions, continue with probability \(q\in(0,1]\). On survival divide the future weight by \(q\). Conditional on the current history and any sampled variables used to choose \(q\),

\[ \mathbb E\left[\mathbf1_{survive}\frac{Y}{q}\right]=Y. \tag{M30} \]

For a scalar future contribution 0.2 and \(q=0.25\), the randomized result is 0.8 with probability 0.25, otherwise zero; its mean is 0.2. Its variance is 0.12. Roulette can increase variance per path while reducing cost enough to improve efficiency per second. It is not a free variance reduction.

A finite maximum scattering depth \(D\) is different: it deterministically removes a nonzero tail. If \(\|T\|\le a<1\), truncating (M15) after \(T^D L_e\) gives the sufficient bound

\[ \left\|L-\sum_{m=0}^{D}T^mL_e\right\| \le\frac{a^{D+1}}{1-a}\|L_e\|. \tag{M31} \]

This bound may be loose; it is not a measured error for an Unreal frame. Finite-depth path-traced images and denoised images must be identified accordingly rather than called exact ground truth.

A complete endpoint sequence

The following algorithm evaluates emitted light at a newly reached endpoint before applying the scattering-depth cap. This permits the BSDF partner of the last allowed NEE sample to reach its emitter. Weighting NEE against a BSDF strategy that the loop never evaluates would undercount even the retained direct term.

ray, beta = sample_normalized_pixel_camera(pixel, rng)
result = 0
scatter_count = 0
previous = none

loop:
    hit = intersect_first(ray)
    if hit is absent:
        # Reconstruct the environment-light PDF at the previous receiver
        # if that environment was also in its NEE light distribution.
        weight = endpoint_mis(previous, environment, ray.direction)
        result += beta * weight * environment_radiance(ray.direction)
        break

    x = hit.position
    wo = -ray.direction
    if hit emits toward wo:
        # First camera hit or previous delta: weight 1.
        # Otherwise use the BSDF-vs-light power weight in one common measure.
        weight = endpoint_mis(previous, hit.emitter, x)
        result += beta * weight * Le(hit, wo)

    if scatter_count >= MAX_SCATTER or hit has no scattering:
        break

    if hit has a connectable continuous BSDF component:
        light_sample = sample_one_light(x, rng)  # includes selection PMF
        if light_sample has valid positive density or mass, and nonzero contribution:
            wi = light_sample.direction
            if segment is visible and BSDF direction pair is valid:
                if light_sample.is_delta_direction:
                    # Point: E_perp = radiant_intensity_toward_x / distance^2.
                    # Directional: E_perp is the specified beam irradiance.
                    # P_light is a discrete light-selection probability.
                    result += beta * fs(x, wi -> wo) * E_perp * cosine_g / P_light
                else:
                    pL = light_sample.solid_angle_pdf
                    pB = continuous_bsdf_pdf(x, wo, wi)
                    wL = power_weight(pL, pB)
                    result += beta * wL * fs(x, wi -> wo) * Li * cosine_g / pL

    sample = sample_bsdf(x, wo, rng)
    if sample is invalid or has zero transport weight:
        break
    # Continuous: weight = fs(wi -> wo) * cosine_g / actual mixture PDF.
    # Delta: weight is derived using the discrete event mass.
    beta *= sample.transport_weight
    previous = { unoffset_x: x, continuous_pdf: sample.pdf,
                 sampled_delta: sample.is_delta, light_sampling_context }
    scatter_count += 1

    if roulette_is_enabled(scatter_count):
        q = choose_positive_survival_probability(beta)
        if uniform() >= q: break
        beta /= q

    ray = robust_spawn_ray(x, sample.direction, hit.geometric_normal)

return result

Here endpoint_mis returns one when there is no competing NEE technique. A delta-direction light sample carries a discrete selection mass and an integrated incident strength, not an ordinary solid-angle PDF; its NEE weight is one against a continuous BSDF directional sampler. Continuous light and BSDF weights use complementary conditional sampling PDFs before roulette; survival compensation is applied to the continuation. Other choices are possible, but a survival probability must not be inserted into one weight and omitted from its complement. Ray-origin offsets change numerical intersection behavior; PDFs and geometry are evaluated at the logical, unoffset vertices. For transmissive paths, the offset side follows the sampled direction.

Put material evaluation in the same integral

Rasterizing a material does not itself integrate illumination. It produces the parameters used to evaluate \(f_s\). A basic opaque model may have diffuse and microfacet components,

\[ f_s=f_d+f_g,\qquad f_d=\frac{\rho_d}{\pi},\qquad f_g(\omega_i,\omega_o)= \frac{D(\mathbf h)F(\omega_i\cdot\mathbf h)G_2(\omega_i,\omega_o)} {4(\mathbf n_s\cdot\omega_i)(\mathbf n_s\cdot\omega_o)}, \tag{M32} \]

where \(\mathbf h=\operatorname{normalize}(\omega_i+\omega_o)\) for reflection in one medium. \(D\) describes microfacet orientations, \(F\) their Fresnel reflection, and \(G_2\) masking and shadowing. The formula assumes a valid reflection pair; it is not a prescription for dividing by zero at the horizon.

For an isotropic GGX distribution with width \(\alpha\),

\[ D(\mathbf h)=\frac{\alpha^2}{\pi\left[(\mathbf n_s\cdot\mathbf h)^2(\alpha^2-1)+1\right]^2}. \tag{M33} \]

Many material models map an artist's perceptual roughness \(r\) to \(\alpha=r^2\). The exact remaps, minimum roughness, masking approximation, energy compensation, and layer rules are implementation choices that must be checked in the shader being analyzed. A simplified Schlick Fresnel is \(F=F_0+(1-F_0)(1-|\omega_i\cdot\mathbf h|)^5\). The historical Epic shading notes explain this family of choices; the engineering chapter checks the installed engine rather than treating a 2013 model as every current shader. Karis, Real Shading in Unreal Engine 4

Sampling the half vector is not the same as sampling a reflected ray. The reflection mapping has Jacobian

\[ p_\sigma(\omega_i)=\frac{p_h(\mathbf h)}{4|\omega_o\cdot\mathbf h|}. \tag{M34} \]

For a visible-normal sampler, \(p_h\) includes visibility from \(\omega_o\), not merely \(D\). Whatever sampler is selected, (M19) divides by its actual directional PDF. Material energy conservation also concerns the sum of lobes: adding a full-strength Lambertian term and an arbitrary full-strength specular term can reflect more incident power than arrives. Metallic workflow, Fresnel coupling, multiple scattering, and layered models determine how Unreal allocates that energy.

Part II · The exact partition and the real-time approximation

At the camera-visible surface, physical path classes can be partitioned into emission, single-scattering source connections, and paths with further scattering. Engine passes partition the workload differently. In particular, Lumen can provide primary-surface sky illumination and illumination from emissive meshes, which are physically direct source connections. It is therefore incorrect to equate “all Lumen output” with “at least two material bounces.”

A useful engineering partition chooses a set \(\mathcal A\) of explicitly handled analytical/local lights. Define \(L_{i,\mathcal A}\) as their direct incident field, and \(L_{i,\mathcal R}\) as the remaining incoming field: environment and other emitter endpoints, plus reflected light from surfaces. Then, for the chosen opaque BSDF,

\[ L_o=L_e+ \int(f_d+f_g)L_{i,\mathcal A}\,d\sigma_x^\perp+ \int f_dL_{i,\mathcal R}\,d\sigma_x^\perp+ \int f_gL_{i,\mathcal R}\,d\sigma_x^\perp. \tag{M35} \]

This is an exact bookkeeping identity after the source sets are defined. A deferred local-light pass approximately evaluates the second term. Lumen diffuse gathering, reflection tracing, and the engine's selected sky/reflection paths approximate parts of the last two. The actual flags determine ownership; (M35) does not assert that every engine configuration uses identical passes.

If an emissive rectangle and an analytical rectangular light both represent the same intended physical lamp, enabling both as independent sources changes the scene's emitted energy unless there is an explicit sampling/representation partition. This is different from adding the same estimator twice. Conversely, the analytical light illuminating a secondary wall, followed by light reflected from that wall toward the primary surface, is a genuinely different path from that light directly illuminating the primary surface.

What deferred rendering changes

For the selected camera samples, rasterization supplies the first visible geometry and material attributes. A G-buffer caches that primary surface state so many lights and secondary-light estimators can reuse it. This changes how visibility and material work are organized, not the radiance integral. Coverage, rasterized geometry, depth precision, stored material precision, and normal encoding are possible approximations.

An exact direct-light estimator would use geometric visibility \(V\) in (M26). Unreal Virtual Shadow Maps store virtualized light-space depth, and their shadow evaluations approximate this visibility and finite-source integration. This VSM is not Variance Shadow Mapping and has no implied Chebyshev-moment formula. Paging, depth rasterization, bias, filtering, and Shadow Map Ray Tracing (SMRT) are separate engineering steps examined with the actual buffers in the engineering chapter. Their common mathematical role is to replace an exact light-connection evaluation with an approximate one.

The diffuse Surface Cache has a concrete mathematical meaning

The installed-engine source audit identifies CombineFinalLighting in Engine/Shaders/Private/Lumen/SurfaceCache/LumenSurfaceCache.ush: decoded albedo multiplies the sum of direct and indirect irradiance by \(1/\pi\), then emission is added. In the scene-linear notation of this chapter,

\[ \widetilde L_{cache}(y)=L_e(y)+ \frac{\rho(y)}{\pi}\left[\widetilde E_{direct}(y)+\widetilde E_{indirect}(y)\right]. \tag{M36} \]

This is a source-audited statement for the pinned engine version, distinct from the abstract model below. The represented final quantity is diffuse outgoing radiance, whereas the two input lighting quantities are irradiance. Their stored numbers also carry a pre-exposure scale. Define \(e_c=\text{CachedLightingPreExposure}\) and use a superscript to distinguish stored values from scene-linear values:

\[ L_{cache}^{\mathrm{pre},c}=e_c\widetilde L_{cache},\qquad E_{direct}^{\mathrm{pre},c}=e_c\widetilde E_{direct},\qquad E_{indirect}^{\mathrm{pre},c}=e_c\widetilde E_{indirect},\qquad L_e^{\mathrm{pre},c}=e_cL_e. \]

The combine shader applies (M36) to those consistently pre-exposed terms. The sampling shader multiplies the sampled atlas values by \(1/e_c\) before returning scene-linear radiance (Engine/Shaders/Private/Lumen/SurfaceCache/LumenSurfaceCacheSampling.ush:314-325). Thus a raw final-lighting texel is not yet the \(\widetilde L_{cache}\) used by the transport integral. Here \(\rho\) means the cache's effective albedo: the card material pass folds a fully rough specular approximation into its diffuse color before encoding it. It is therefore not always the original material's diffuse albedo, particularly for metallic materials. The representation omits the full outgoing directional dependence of a general glossy BSDF.

The screen-probe integration output has a different normalization. In the audited baseline, LumenScreenProbeGather.usf multiplies integrated diffuse lighting by Diffuse_Lambert(1) before writing the diffuse-indirect output. Its physical quantity is a unit-albedo diffuse response, \(E/\pi\); its stored value carries the view pre-exposure \(e_v=\text{View.PreExposure}\), so \(D^{\mathrm{pre},v}=e_vE/\pi\). The trace has already applied this view scale, and DiffuseIndirectComposite.usf later multiplies the response by the receiver's diffuse color. Dividing it by \(\pi\) again, or applying \(e_v\) a second time in the composite, would be an error. The card irradiance atlases, card final radiance, and screen \(E/\pi\) response therefore need distinct quantity and exposure labels in every visualization.

Suppose a visible diffuse point \(x\) gathers the cached analytical direct lighting at a secondary point \(y\). Its contribution includes

\[ \frac{\rho(x)}{\pi} \int \left[\frac{\rho(y)}{\pi}\widetilde E_{direct}(y)\right] G(x\leftrightarrow y)\,dA(y). \tag{M37} \]

Equation (M37) uses scene-linear \(\widetilde E_{direct}=E_{direct}^{\mathrm{pre},c}/e_c\); substituting the raw pre-exposed atlas value would introduce an extra scale. There are two scattering factors, one at \(y\) and one at \(x\). Subtracting the secondary cache's direct lighting as a duplicate of the primary deferred direct pass would delete legitimate bounced light. In contrast, sky or emitter radiance reached directly from \(x\) has only the primary scattering factor. These examples distinguish path classification from buffer names.

An original operator model for cache approximation

The next equations are an explanatory model of discretization and reuse, not a claim that Epic implements a literal global matrix solve. Let \(P\) project a radiance field to finite cache coefficients and \(R\) reconstruct a field, with \(PR=I\) on coefficient space. Let \(T_h\) be an approximate transport operator using represented geometry, materials, and visibility. Define

\[ K=P T_h R,\qquad b_c=P b_h,\qquad c=b_c+Kc,\qquad \widetilde L=Rc. \tag{M38} \]

The reduced coefficients can represent card texels, probe directional bins, or another explicitly chosen basis. Direction-independent diffuse cards and directional probes are different bases; they must not be silently identified. Relative to exact \(L^*=b+TL^*\), define reconstructed source \(\widetilde b=R b_c\), operator \(\widetilde T=RKP\), and current residual

\[ \widetilde r=\widetilde b+\widetilde T\widetilde L-\widetilde L. \tag{M39} \]

Subtracting the current approximate state from the exact fixed-point equation gives

\[ (I-T)(L^*-\widetilde L) =(b-\widetilde b)+(T-\widetilde T)\widetilde L+\widetilde r. \tag{M40} \]

If \(\|T\|<1\),

\[ \|L^*-\widetilde L\| \le\frac{\|b-\widetilde b\|+ \|(T-\widetilde T)\widetilde L\|+\|\widetilde r\|}{1-\|T\|}. \tag{M41} \]

The three terms separate source representation error, transport/representation error, and an unfinished or stale solve. More ray samples can reduce stochastic noise without making coarse cards, missing geometry, or stale history exact. Nonlinear clipping, adaptive scheduling, and view-dependent reconstruction require additional analysis; they are not covered by pretending that (M38) is the entire production renderer.

A two-patch transport example. With dimensionless coupling matrix and source radiances

\[ T=\begin{bmatrix}0&0.2\\0.3&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad b=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}, \tag{M42} \]

the exact solution is \(L^*=(1,0.3)^T/0.94=(1.063829787,0.319148936)^T\). Starting at \(b\), repeated substitution produces \((1,0)\), \((1,0.3)\), \((1.06,0.3)\), \((1.06,0.318)\), and so on. If a material/transport approximation changes the second coupling to 0.25, its converged solution is \((1,0.25)^T/0.95=(1.052631579,0.263157895)^T\). Infinite iterations remove its residual but do not remove its difference from the original scene. This small algebraic example demonstrates approximation error; it is not an Unreal measurement or a speed benchmark.

Reconstruction and the cost that is actually removed

Sparse gathering replaces per-pixel directional integration with samples at selected probe positions. A generic reconstruction is

\[ \widetilde L_i(x,\omega)=\sum_m a_m(x)\widehat L_i(x_m,\omega), \qquad \sum_m a_m(x)=1. \tag{M43} \]

Normalization preserves a constant field, but does not prove that a field varying across depth discontinuities is reconstructed correctly. The samples are taken at different positions. Similarly, an exponential history update

\[ H_t=(1-\alpha)H_{t-1}^{reprojected}+\alpha\widehat L_t \tag{M44} \]

can preserve the mean of a stationary correctly reprojected field while retaining lag after lighting changes. Disocclusion, clamping, and invalid-history rejection change its behavior. Temporal reuse is not intrinsically biased in every static case; production reuse and reconstruction can nevertheless introduce persistent spatial and temporal error.

A simple reference path tracer costs approximately \(N_{pixels}N_{samples}\bar d\) continuation intersections, plus shadow connections, material evaluations, and acceleration-structure work. A sparse gather uses roughly \(N_{probes}N_{directions}\) initial queries plus its cache updates, filtering, and reconstruction. Reusing a secondary hit's cached material/lighting also avoids repeating the full material and light evaluation for every ray. These are the mechanisms of reduced work. Counts alone are not a speedup measurement: screen hits, compacted misses, distance-field steps, hardware traversal, cache misses, and asynchronous work have different costs. The captured dispatch sizes and timings must establish the actual savings.

The diffuse and glossy terms of (M35) also require different angular detail. A rough glossy lobe can reuse a low-frequency incident-radiance representation; a narrow lobe needs more directional detail or a dedicated ray. If two routes approximate the same glossy term, an exclusive selection or a defined blend \(wC_1+(1-w)C_2\) avoids simply adding that term twice. A roughness threshold does not by itself turn a finite glossy BRDF into a physical delta distribution. Source inspection must determine whether a threshold selects a representation, blends estimators, clamps a lobe, or actually changes the BSDF.

For example, a generic normalized bilateral reconstruction can be written

\[ \widetilde L_i(x,\omega)= \frac{\sum_m \chi_m(x)\,b_m(x)\,\widehat L_i(x_m,\omega)} {\sum_m \chi_m(x)\,b_m(x)},\qquad b_m=w_{space,m}w_{depth,m}w_{normal,m}. \tag{M45} \]

An all-invalid neighborhood is not “black lighting” unless that is explicitly the algorithm's chosen fallback. Similarly, history blending is only reproducible when the reprojected sample, rejection tests, exposure conversion, clamp bounds, and effective weight are available. These are computational inputs, not decorative debug overlays.
