Give the estimator a frame budget

A path tracer can render Sponza without cards or probes. At 1920×1080, with 64 paths per pixel and an average of four traced segments per path, it would issue about 531 million intersection queries before adding shadow connections. That is a hypothetical workload. At one path per pixel the cost falls, but noise flickers in the shaded arcade and in narrow glossy reflections.

Neighboring pixels often land on the same patch of stone. Rays from elsewhere in the arcade can hit the same courtyard wall, and a stationary wall may receive similar light in the next frame. A rough surface also averages light over a broad range of directions. Cache material and lighting where rays hit a surface. Near a receiver, nearby shading points can share measurements of incoming directional light. Across frames, a previous measurement can save another sample. Each kind of reuse needs a test for when the old value still applies.

Keep the contributions separate before approximating them

At the visible point, split the BSDF into diffuse and glossy components, \(f_s=f_d+f_g\). Let A be a set of explicit analytic lights, such as the sun and a rectangular test light, whose direct contributions the direct-lighting path evaluates. Let \(L_{i,A}\) hold their direct incident field and \(L_{i,R}\) hold the remaining incoming field. R includes light reflected from surfaces, and it can also include direct sky and emissive-mesh connections. Then

\[ L_o=L_e+ \underbrace{\int(f_d+f_g)L_{i,A}\,d\sigma_x^\perp}_{\text{explicit direct lights}} +\underbrace{\int f_d L_{i,R}\,d\sigma_x^\perp}_{\text{remaining diffuse}} +\underbrace{\int f_g L_{i,R}\,d\sigma_x^\perp}_{\text{remaining glossy}}. \]

Once the source sets are defined, this partition is exact. Approximation begins with how each term is evaluated: rasterization supplies the primary visible point, deferred lighting and shadow maps handle explicit direct lights, and Lumen estimates and reconstructs much of the remaining illumination. The renderer's names “direct” and “indirect” describe responsibilities; they do not reliably count scattering vertices. Lumen, for example, can supply a direct sky contribution at the primary surface.

Follow sunlight that first hits courtyard stone y and then illuminates arcade floor x. The direct-lighting result at x does not include that path, so the cached direct lighting at y is a legitimate input to indirect illumination at x. Removing it because “direct lighting was already added” would delete real reflected light. Conversely, if an emissive mesh and an analytic light both represent the same lamp's energy, the scene author must define which one owns that energy; two independent emitters can double the physical source before any estimator is evaluated.

Which changes are unbiased, and which change the answer?

Importance sampling and correctly weighted roulette change the distribution or amount of work but preserve the intended expectation. Spatial interpolation, directional compression, simplified geometry, temporal clipping, and diffuse secondary-hit shading generally change the estimated field. More samples reduce Monte Carlo noise around that changed field; they do not automatically recover the original scene.

The exact transport problem can be written as \(L=b+TL\), where b is emission and T is one scattering-and-propagation operation. Expanding it gives \(L=b+Tb+T^2b+\cdots\), the sequence of paths derived earlier. A finite cache stores coefficients c; let P project a field into those coefficients and R reconstruct it. One explanatory cached update is

\[ c_{t+1}=P b_h+P T_h R c_t,\qquad \widetilde L_t=R c_t. \]

The subscript h indicates represented geometry and material. This is a model for reasoning about approximation; it does not claim that Epic stores a global matrix or runs a uniform Jacobi solver. The actual system updates selected pages and probes with different histories and budgets. Still, the model exposes three distinct errors: a wrong represented source, a wrong transport/reconstruction operator, and an incomplete or stale update.

Derive a bound on the approximation error

For a stationary linear teaching model, write the reconstructed fixed point as L̃ = b̃ + T̃L̃, with b̃ = RPbₕ and T̃ = RPTₕ. Subtract the exact equation L = b + TL and collect the unknown difference:

\[ (I-\widetilde T)(\widetilde L-L) =(\widetilde b-b)+(\widetilde T-T)L. \]

If the chosen operator norm satisfies ‖T̃‖ < 1, its inverse expands as a convergent geometric series. Therefore

\[ \|\widetilde L-L\|\le \frac{\|\widetilde b-b\|+\|\widetilde T-T\|\,\|L\|}{1-\|\widetilde T\|}. \]

The numerator separates changed sources from changed transport, and the denominator shows why repeated interreflection can amplify a local error. The bound is conditional, not a measured Lumen error bar: production clamping, visibility decisions, moving scenes, and adaptive scheduling are not one stationary linear operator. It describes what a convergence test can establish under a precisely stated model. At an unfinished iteration, add the difference between its current state and this approximate fixed point; stochastic sampling also contributes variance around the update.

Two surfaces are enough to distinguish the errors. Let \(L_1=1+0.2L_2\) and \(L_2=0.3L_1\). Starting from (1,0), successive substitutions give (1,0.3), (1.06,0.3), (1.06,0.318), and converge to approximately (1.06383,0.31915). If a visibility or material approximation changes the second coefficient to 0.25, the converged result becomes approximately (1.05263,0.26316). Both iterations stabilize; only the first solves the original pair of equations.

A useful debugging order follows from this. Establish that the queried geometry and source exist, then check the meaning and exposure of the stored values, then inspect interpolation and history validity. Only after those contracts are correct should you read a sample-count increase as a convergence test.

Start at update zero, then advance. Each step uses both values from the previous step, so this is a Jacobi iteration. Switch the coupling to 0.25: the sequence still converges, but to a different answer. More updates remove this toy model's iteration error; they do not repair its altered transport. Actual Lumen has interleaved updates and selected page budgets.

Construct and decode the GBuffer

A minimal implementation stores depth, a shading normal, diffuse or base color, roughness, metallic, specular or F₀ information, a shading-model identity, and velocity for later reprojection. Emissive output may accumulate into SceneColor through the base-pass contract instead of occupying a universal “emissive GBuffer.” The exact layout depends on platform and shader permutation. Decode it with the producer's layout; do not infer channel meanings from a screenshot's colors.

Material evaluation runs before the GBuffer write. Vertex attributes and interpolated texture coordinates enter the compiled material program; texture samples, constants, and graph operations produce material properties, and tangent-space normals are transformed into the shading frame. Opaque coverage is selected with the depth test. Masked geometry must apply its opacity decision in every representation that needs consistent visibility. Normalized textures, sRGB decoding, tangent handedness, normal normalization, and roughness remapping are computational inputs, not cosmetic details.

Depth is usually device depth, not distance in metres. Reconstruct the point with the appropriate inverse view-projection transform and homogeneous divide. In Unreal, reversed depth, translated-world coordinates, view-rectangle offsets, temporal jitter, and large-world origins all affect the result. In schematic form:

uv = (pixel + sample_offset - view_rect_min) / view_rect_size
clip = make_clip_position(uv, device_depth, projection_convention)
position_h = inverse_view_projection * clip
position = position_h.xyz / position_h.w
normal = decode_and_normalize_gbuffer_normal(pixel)
material = decode_material(pixel, shading_model_id)
outgoing = normalize(camera_position - position)

A half-resolution lighting buffer must map back through the correct full-resolution view rectangle. A shifted depth lookup can look like bad GI at edges when the real problem is addressing. The reference's decoded GBuffer records preserve the actual channel layout and pixel arithmetic of the instrumented room frame.

Direct lighting and the shadows each light casts

Start at courtyard point A. A direct connection joins the light to A and then A to the camera, without another scattering surface between the light and A. The GBuffer already supplies A's position, normal, material, and outgoing camera direction. Direct lighting adds the other half: which lights reach A, how much incident energy they supply, whether geometry blocks them, and how A scatters that energy toward the camera.

A direct connection says nothing about the BRDF. The same analytic light can produce both a broad diffuse response and a narrow specular highlight. A reflection of a sunlit wall, by contrast, contains a secondary surface interaction and belongs to the scene-reflection path. Without that separation, a direct highlight can be added once by deferred lighting and again by an overlapping reflection estimate.

Turn each light type into an incident-light query

For each relevant analytic light, determine its direction or finite-source distribution, radiometric scale, attenuation and support, visibility, and the material response. The point-light ideal is proportional to intensity divided by squared distance. Engine attenuation also includes finite influence and source-shape conventions. A finite rectangle subtends an angular region; evaluating it requires an integral or an approximation such as the engine's area-light treatment. It is not physically equivalent to a point source just because both share a light center.

For ideal point and directional lights, let \(E_\ell^\perp(x)\) denote the incident irradiance on a plane perpendicular to the light direction at x. For a point source of radiant intensity \(I_\ell\), it is \(I_\ell/r^2\); for an ideal directional source it does not decay with receiver distance. With the common geometric/shading normal n used in our first model, the owned direct contribution is

\[ L_{\rm direct}(x,\omega_o)=\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal L_D} f_s(x;\omega_\ell\!\to\!\omega_o)\, E_\ell^\perp(x)\,\max(0,n\cdot\omega_\ell)\,V_\ell(x). \]

This writes the directional delta source through its integrated weight, rather than pretending it has an ordinary finite radiance density over solid angle. A spotlight additionally restricts the angular support and varies intensity within its cone. A production local light also has a finite influence bound and source-shape parameters. A finite area source returns to the area/solid-angle integral derived earlier: different source points can have different directions, distances, BRDF values, and visibility. Replacing that integral with one center sample changes the target, especially near large sources.

Check the multiplication before building the shadow system. Give a Lambertian receiver reflectance 0.6 and zero emission. Let one prescribed light supply perpendicular irradiance 10 W m⁻², with incident cosine 0.5 and visibility estimate 0.75. The irradiance reaching the surface is 10 × 0.5 × 0.75 = 3.75 W m⁻². Its outgoing diffuse radiance is (0.6/π) × 3.75 ≈ 0.7162 W m⁻² sr⁻¹. A specular material adds the corresponding specular-BRDF response to this same owned source. This analytic example tests units and factor placement; the Sponza values below use Unreal's recorded RGB calibration.

Evaluate the material at the receiver

A basic direct-light implementation can start with point and directional lights, exact shadow rays, and the diffuse-plus-GGX BSDF already derived. Then replace only the visibility query with a shadow-map query. That gives a controlled before/after test: the BSDF and source stay fixed while the visibility representation changes. Tiled or clustered light lists avoid evaluating every scene light at every pixel. These lists are culling structures, so a false negative loses illumination; a conservative false positive merely costs work.

for light_id in lights_overlapping_pixel_or_cluster:
    light = decode_light(light_id)
    incident = evaluate_light_geometry_and_radiance(light, position)
    visibility = evaluate_shadow(light, position, normal)
    brdf = evaluate_diffuse_plus_specular(material, incident.wi, outgoing)
    scene_color += brdf * incident.radiometric_factor * visibility

The factor in this pseudocode includes the incident cosine and whichever source integration convention the chosen light uses. A production function may return a preintegrated lighting contribution rather than raw radiance. Its units and cosine placement must be documented before multiplying it by another helper.

In a deferred implementation, decode the shading-model identity before interpreting its material payload. A Default Lit metal uses its base color primarily as colored specular reflectance; a dielectric retains a diffuse color and its interface reflectance. Compute the light/view half-vector and the selected distribution, visibility, and Fresnel terms in the same normalized shading frame. The source may fold the microfacet denominator into a visibility helper and may integrate a finite source analytically. Multiplying a textbook GGX denominator around that helper a second time is a different BRDF. The earlier microfacet derivation supplies the target; the selected shader supplies its implementation convention.

Light culling and light evaluation are also separate. A light volume, screen rectangle, tile list, or cluster list decides which receivers might be affected. Attenuation, cone support, lighting channels, light functions, and shadowing decide the contribution after that conservative selection. The captured Sponza sun uses a standard deferred light pass. The teaching loop describes ownership and arithmetic; it does not claim that this particular sun was dispatched through a tiled light-list kernel.

Follow that multiplication in the captured legacy GBuffer route: the deferred lighting accumulator computes local attenuation, obtains the applicable shadow terms, calls IntegrateBxDF for the selected source shape, applies the light's diffuse/specular scales, and accumulates the split result with attenuated light color and surface shadow. That helper's source integration is already part of the returned lighting. The example capture has Substrate disabled; other material permutations require their own decode and integration contracts.

Construct the shadow views from the light

We know which surface the camera sees. Shadowing asks a different visibility question: what lies between that surface and a particular light? A shadow map answers by rasterizing the scene from a light-dependent view. Its texels store the nearest represented blocker along that view's rays. The primary camera supplies receivers that need answers; it is not the camera used to render the shadow depth.

A directional light needs position-dependent coverage, not perspective foreshortening

An ideal directional light supplies parallel incident rays. Build an orthonormal light-space frame with two transverse axes a and b and a depth axis. In a teaching orthographic view centered at c, with transverse half-width R, a world point x maps to

\[ u=\frac12+\frac{a\cdot(x-c)}{2R},\qquad v=\frac12-\frac{b\cdot(x-c)}{2R}. \]

The sign on v is a chosen texture-coordinate convention. Unlike perspective projection, there is no division by distance along the light ray. A column does not shrink in the sun's shadow image merely because it is farther from an arbitrarily chosen light-view origin. We still need a finite depth interval that includes relevant casters, a world-to-light transform, and a finite transverse region to store.

One enormous orthographic map would spend the same texel density everywhere. Directional VSM clipmaps instead provide nested light-space coverage around the view, with increasing world-space footprint at coarser levels. A receiver selects a suitable level; virtual pages provide sparse storage within that level. Clipmap level and texture mip level are different indices. In the captured sun, IDs 8192–8208 represent clip levels 6–22; the storage example below uses ID 8196, clip level 10, at logical mip 0.

A stable light-space grid matters as much as resolution, because reuse depends on it. If the grid followed every subtexel camera translation continuously, the same stationary edge would move across depth texels every frame. Snapping the clipmap to that grid and maintaining it makes reuse possible. When coverage scrolls, the system can retain overlapping world regions and request newly exposed pages, provided the represented light transform, depth range, and validity state remain compatible.

The actual clipmap placement snaps the light-space center to a level-dependent grid. The reversed-Z orthographic matrix sets its transverse extent and depth range, while receiver level selection begins with the logarithm of distance from the clipmap origin. This is a spatial resolution policy, not a change to the physical direction of the sun.

Local lights need perspective directions and a finite influence bound

For a local source at o, let q = x − o and d be its component along a selected shadow face's forward axis. If θ is that face's horizontal half-angle, its horizontal coordinate in a symmetric perspective view is

\[ u=\frac12+\frac{a\cdot q}{2d\tan\theta}. \]

The vertical coordinate has the corresponding vertical half-angle and texture-axis sign. This division by d is what lets rays diverge from a point. A spot light uses its orientation and outer cone to choose a perspective frustum. Its attenuation radius bounds how far the implementation searches and casts; the outer cone bounds angular support. The inner cone changes light intensity, so it should not be mistaken for the boundary of the shadow view.

A point light illuminates directions around its position. Six perspective views, facing ±X, ±Y and ±Z, cover those directions as cube faces. Each face has its own virtual-page region and projection. The selected direction determines the face; a point close to a face boundary needs consistent face selection and filtering, or the shadow develops seams. The light's influence radius also limits the caster and receiver region. This finite bound is a rendering policy; an ideal mathematical point source's inverse-square field does not become exactly zero at that radius.

The pinned engine's rectangular-light whole-scene shadow initializer also selects the one-pass point-light/cubemap route. This does not turn the rectangular emitter into a physical point source: its finite shape still matters to direct-light evaluation, while its VSM soft-shadow path uses an additional approximation. The stored visibility representation is therefore built from that selected set of projection views. A true area-light visibility integral would ask for visibility from many different emitter positions. VSM soft-shadow estimation uses the stored depth representation to approximate those connections, rather than rasterizing a separate complete shadow map for every point on the emitter.

That approximation is concrete in this version: the rect light supplies half its width as SourceRadius and half its height as SourceLength. The inspected local VSM ray generator samples a disk using SourceRadius; it does not sample the rectangle using both dimensions. Thus the stored cube depth and the disk-based soft-shadow estimate are each approximations, separate from the rectangle used by the direct-light BRDF integral. Follow the rect shadow initializer, rect source parameters, and local SMRT ray generator. Do not generalize this contract to a separate ray-traced area-shadow implementation.

The local-light implementations make the bounds concrete: the spot initializer uses inverse tangent of the outer cone, while point and rectangular initializers select the cubemap path. All three retain a near limit and a maximum casting distance. These parameters belong in the projection record alongside the light identity; they cannot be inferred from a depth texture's width and height. The spot initializer supplies the cone scale; the six-face setup supplies face directions, up vectors, and a 90-degree perspective matrix with near depth 1 and far depth equal to the light radius. Those are the actual cube-projection values, even though the earlier initializer also carries its own minimum-light-depth field.

Rasterize casters into the requested depth pages

Start with an ordinary nonvirtual shadow map. For each eligible caster triangle, apply its current object deformation and transform, then the light-view and projection matrices. Clip against the light frustum, rasterize the projected triangle, and evaluate any required masked-material coverage. At each covered depth texel, retain the nearest blocker in the chosen depth convention. The material's albedo and final shaded color do not belong in this depth image. Material coverage and vertex motion can still change which fragments reach it.

Let d be positive forward depth in a perspective shadow view with near and far depths denoted by the subscripts “near” and “far”. A reversed-Z teaching projection has the form A + B/d. Requiring the near plane to map to 1 and the far plane to 0 gives

\[ z(d)=\frac{d_{\rm near}d_{\rm far}}{(d_{\rm far}-d_{\rm near})d}-\frac{d_{\rm near}}{d_{\rm far}-d_{\rm near}}. \]

For an orthographic view the corresponding mapping is linear, z(d) = (far − d)/(far − near). These formulas describe projection depth, not linear radiance. Closer blockers have larger z under this convention. A clear value of zero represents the far end of an otherwise valid empty depth image. With a positive floating-point depth encoding, unsigned integer ordering preserves the order of those depth values; this lets the virtual-depth raster path use an integer maximum operation on their bit patterns. The sampling path later reinterprets the word as a float.

Now virtualize the same operation. Receivers mark needed light-space pages and resolution levels. Allocation supplies physical tiles and their state. Caster culling determines which scene instances and triangles overlap those requested page frusta. The raster path translates each covered virtual texel to its physical tile and writes depth there. Nanite and conventional geometry have different submission, culling, and raster implementations, but both must satisfy the same address, depth, and validity contract before projection samples the result. In the conventional VSM depth pixel shader, a decoded page must have rendering validity before InterlockedMax writes asuint(DeviceZ) to the translated address. The static-page bit chooses the static cache slice; other depth goes to slice zero.

For an ideal single depth comparison at receiver x, a simple reversed-Z visibility estimate is

\[ \widetilde V(x)=\mathbf 1\!\left[z_s(u(x),v(x))\le z_r(x)+\epsilon(x)\right]. \]

Here zₛ is the stored blocker depth, zᵣ is the receiver depth in the same projection, and ε represents a declared comparison bias in these depth units. A positive bias makes this simple test more permissive. The production path has receiver-normal, slope, footprint, and soft-shadow rules that must be followed in their own units and order. Increasing ε arbitrarily can hide acne by detaching shadows and admitting light through thin blockers.

A finite source produces partial visibility because different source points can be blocked differently. Filtering a depth representation is therefore an approximation to a visibility integral. SMRT walks selected rays through shadow-map depths to estimate that soft visibility. A single stored nearest depth does not reveal all the occluders behind it; more filter samples cannot recover a layer the representation never stored. The later captured mask is the output of that process, not simply a grayscale copy of the depth pool.

Moving lights change the meaning of cached depth

Imagine moving a point light sideways while leaving every wall fixed. The same wall projects to different cube-face texels, and its depth changes relative to the new light origin. Keeping the old depth pages and merely replacing the light matrix would compare new receiver coordinates against old caster coordinates. A moved local light therefore requires compatible cache invalidation and refreshed shadow views. Rotating a directional light similarly changes the light-space projection of the entire scene. The pinned cache keys contain WorldToLight, PreShadowTranslation, and distant-light status for a local light, and light direction plus first level for a directional clipmap. Changing a key invalidates the rendered-frame state; the subsequent uncached render establishes reusable contents.

Moving only the primary camera is a different operation. It changes visible receiver demand and directional-clipmap coverage, while a stationary light and stationary caster can still describe the same world-space depth in overlapping valid pages. A moving caster instead invalidates the pages intersected by its old/new affected bounds. Deformation, world-position offset, masked coverage changes, and changes to shadow-casting participation also belong in the invalidation policy. Updating an object's transform without updating its shadow dependencies leaves an apparently stable but stale cache. The implementation projects affected instance bounds into allocated pages and sets invalidation flags there. For camera motion, clipmap reuse retains the cached depth interval and records a page-address offset while the depth guard band and other validity tests remain satisfied; it invalidates the level when they do not.

Separate static and dynamic depth storage can preserve static geometry while dynamic geometry is cleared and rendered again, then combine the required depths for sampling. Here the static/dynamic merge takes the maximum of the two stored reversed-Z words, so the nearer represented blocker wins; the merge is dispatched for pages selected by the corresponding dirty-state path. A physical allocation, an initialized tile, a cacheable tile, and a tile whose contents are valid for the current projection are distinct states. The storage section below decodes those records independently. Its request counter uses a scene-frame numbering domain; it must not be substituted for a screen-probe history count or the frame number shown in another capture.

Changing only a light's intensity or color does not geometrically change the ideal shadow depth. It changes the lighting multiplied by visibility. A cache key can encode that distinction, even if a production update path conservatively invalidates more state after a light-proxy change. Position, orientation, shape-dependent projection, influence bounds, and caster state change the depth geometry; intensity and color do not.

A light or caster can be outside the primary image

First consider an off-screen light. Its source position may project outside the image while its influence volume overlaps visible floor or walls. The renderer must retain it for those receivers. A point-light sphere, spot cone, or other conservative influence bound is a culling object, so the source's screen coordinate alone is not an adequate culling test. A directional light's influence is not restricted to the pixels containing a visible sun disk.

Next consider an off-screen caster. A column behind the camera can stand between a visible floor point and a local light. The floor marks a page in the light's projection. Rendering that page must consider eligible geometry in the corresponding light-space caster region, including geometry omitted from the primary camera's color and depth buffers. Raster shadow maps can therefore represent this shadow even though a screen-depth-only trace cannot see the column. Culling casters solely against the primary view would be a correctness error.

Finally consider an off-screen receiver. It creates no primary opaque-pixel demand in this view, but the renderer may need broader/coarser shadow coverage for other consumers, such as translucent lighting and volumetrics. Those consumers have their own request and sampling policies. An opaque screen receiver's fine page requests do not prove that every off-screen secondary surface has equally fine coverage. In Lumen, card lighting and hit-lighting paths must use their selected shadow-query route; they do not acquire a correct shadow simply by copying the primary camera's shadow-mask pixel.

The source keeps receiver and caster work separate: local receiver marking queries the culled light grid at visible depth, then applies radius, cone, and rect-facing tests; local caster culling uses the light's bounds and caster frustum. Directional coarse pages and local coarse pages provide broader coverage for their consumers. This page-marking path has no Lumen-card receiver input. An off-screen blocker can therefore cast a visible shadow, and fine primary-view coverage cannot be assumed for every secondary surface.

Virtual Shadow Maps: spend depth storage where receivers need it

A shadow map looks at geometry from a light and stores the nearest depth. Transform a receiver into the light's projection and compare its depth with the stored depth. If it lies behind the nearest occluder, that light connection is blocked. In Sponza, balcony rails and columns create rapidly changing visibility, so a low-resolution uniform shadow map loses detail while a huge one wastes storage elsewhere.

Virtual Shadow Maps separate a large logical address space from a smaller pool of physical depth pages. Visible receivers mark required pages at appropriate resolution. The system allocates physical pages, reuses valid cached ones, invalidates pages affected by moving geometry or lights, culls geometry into the requested pages, and renders their depth. A lookup translates the virtual address through the page table before reading the physical tile. Directional-light clipmaps supply scale and coverage around the view; local lights use their appropriate projections and mip levels.

Build a minimal virtual implementation in this order: a fixed light-space depth image; a tiled image with an identity page table; receiver-driven page requests; physical allocation with residency/validity bits; cached pages with explicit invalidation; then clipmaps or mips. At every stage compare the resulting visibility against the nonvirtual image. Otherwise allocation errors become indistinguishable from shadow bias errors.

The pinned renderer exposes these handoffs in page marking, allocation, and shadow rendering. After rendering and its page-state updates, the projection entry points distinguish local-light projections from directional clipmaps and composite their visibility masks. Allocating an address alone does not establish that its depth is valid for sampling.

Depth bias prevents the receiver's own numerical depth error from becoming a false occluder. Too much bias separates shadows from their casters and permits leaks. Filtering approximates the variation of visibility across a footprint or finite source. Unreal's Shadow Map Ray Tracing (SMRT) samples virtual shadow depths along rays used for soft-shadow estimation. It still works with a depth representation that may not contain every occluder layer. Missing layers, thin geometry, projection boundaries, and finite sample counts explain artifacts that a higher lighting sample count cannot fix. “VSM” here means Virtual Shadow Maps, not Variance Shadow Mapping.

Rendering must happen before a newly allocated page becomes sampleable. A page can be resident but dirty; allocation alone does not mean its old contents describe this light. A finite memory pool also needs a defined overflow policy. Unmapped pages must produce a diagnosed fallback, not an uninitialized read. Keep request count, allocation count, cached reuse count, dirty count, and overflow count visible during development.

For a conventional depth that increases away from the light, a minimal comparison is visible if receiverDepth ≤ storedDepth + bias. Reverse the ordering when the actual stored depth convention reverses it. For a finite area source, the ideal direct estimate integrates visibility over source points. Filtering and SMRT approximate that varying visibility using the depth representation and finite samples. They do not turn a single depth layer into a complete geometric model of every source-to-receiver segment.

Read the storage before interpreting the shadow

A shadow mask is the answer to a visibility query. The physical depth pool is one of the data structures used to obtain that answer. Its neighboring tiles need not depict neighboring pieces of Sponza: the allocator places whichever virtual pages need storage into available physical slots. To recover a light-space image, we must follow the page table in the opposite direction, gathering the physical tiles into their virtual locations.

Three separate questions determine what a sample means. Which virtual address did this light projection request? Which physical tile currently backs that address? Which depth values in that tile are valid for this query? The page table answers the second; flags and cache metadata constrain the third. A bright page-table color is an address category, not a bright light or a nearby occluder.

The physical pool is 16,384×4,096 texels, with two captured array slices. Projection samples slice 0; the separately cached static depth uses slice 1 in this configuration. Each slice occupies 256 MiB at four bytes per texel. Free slots provide no shadow answers until a mapping and valid contents back them. The smaller table and metadata let the renderer decide which parts of that memory represent a requested light-space region.

A directional fallback in this table selects a coarser clipmap level, still sampled at logical mip 0. For a local shadow map, the corresponding LOD mechanism can select a coarser logical mip of that map. An address translation must know which policy it is applying. The large regions of the full map above are repeated fallback references, not thousands of separately rendered physical tiles.

Logical shadow mips, hardware texture mips, and hierarchical flags

A full VSM's logical mip 0 has 128×128 page entries. Logical mips 1–7 have 64², 32², 16², 8², 4², 2², and 1² entries. All eight regions fit inside one 128×192 block of the page-table texture's hardware mip 0. Relative to that block, mip 0 begins at (0,0); the tail begins at Y=128, with X offsets 0, 64, 96, 112, 120, 124, and 126 for mips 1–7. The selected map's block begins at global texel (640,0). Thus its logical-mip-zero page (63,65) occupies table texel (703,65).

The logical-level packing is separate from the PageFlags texture's hardware mip pyramid. Each PageFlags word uses the low six bits for allocation, dynamic uncached, static uncached, detail geometry, primary request, and secondary request. The hierarchy update ORs those flags into coarser texture mips at shifted addresses. It does not pack eight six-bit levels into a 32-bit word. The hierarchy lets a culling operation ask whether a larger page region contains relevant work without inspecting every leaf.

The page size in this source version is 128×128 texels. For a nonnegative integer virtual texel address u at an already selected level, split each coordinate into a page coordinate p and a local coordinate q:

\[ u=128p+q,\qquad p=\lfloor u/128\rfloor,\qquad q=u\bmod128. \]

Read the page-table entry for this shadow-map identity, level, and p. If it supplies a valid physical page coordinate a at this same level, the physical texel address is t = 128a + q. The offset within a page survives the translation; only the page's location changes. A coarser fallback also changes the sampling level and virtual coordinates, so the same-level formula must not be applied blindly to an entry with a nonzero LOD offset.

What a physical depth texel actually stores

The projection shader's physical load reads an unsigned 32-bit word from pool layer zero and reinterprets its bits as a floating-point depth; it does not divide the integer by the largest possible integer. The integer representation supports the renderer's storage and raster operations, while the interpreted floating-point value participates in the light-space depth test.

Every selected-depth view uses the same range, 0.4998…0.5003, with color coordinate t = (z − 0.4998)/0.0005. B's stored depth exceeds its receiver depth by about 0.000271428, consistent with a blocker closer to the light in reversed Z. It is not a proof that one center test reproduces SMRT: the actual ray directions, jitter, slope and normal handling, and individual sample decisions are not present in this memory snapshot.

A depth value belongs to its light projection and level. It is neither camera depth nor a distance in centimeters by itself. Clear depth inside a valid mapped page and storage in an unallocated page are also different states. The first can mean that no occluder was written at that texel; the second has no usable visibility interpretation. The visualization keeps those categories separate instead of turning both into black.

Follow the shadow result into the direct-light addition

The mask texture stores square-root-encoded attenuation. Decode its red byte c as (c/255)²; treating c/255 as linear would brighten partially shadowed samples. At A the byte is 255 and the consumed attenuation is 1. At B and C it is 0. The sun pass adds pre-exposed RGB (1.9306641, 1.3037109, 0.7900391) at A and exactly (0,0,0) at B and C. Thus the gold sphere can be bright in the final image while receiving no contribution from this analytic sun at that particular point: its reflected incident field belongs to another term of our partition.

The encode/decode functions establish this square-root convention. The mask is a packed set of attenuation channels: the red channel carries this sun's whole-scene directional shadow, while other channels serve subsurface, per-object, or light-function terms in applicable branches. Decode the channels according to the selected light path; do not treat every four-channel shadow texture as four copies of one scalar visibility.

Tracing geometry outside the camera view

An incoming-light query begins in the shaded arcade and follows a sampled direction. It may hit a column, pass into the courtyard, or miss toward the sky. Tracing every query against full triangle geometry is expensive. The depth buffer already holds detailed visible surfaces, including the rasterized material geometry, so begin there.

First try screen depth, but define what a hit proves

Project points along the ray into screen space and compare the ray's depth with scene depth. A hierarchy of depth bounds lets traversal skip large screen regions when no intersection is possible and descend near a candidate crossing. The resulting hierarchical Z buffer (HZB) stores min/max or conservative bounds, depending on its depth convention. With reversed depth, the intuitive meaning of "larger" flips. The comparison must follow the actual producer.

A depth crossing is not automatically a valid world hit. The screen stores frontmost visible surfaces, not closed volumes. A thickness test determines whether the crossing is plausibly near the represented surface. Edge uncertainty, missing back layers, view boundaries, and unsupported materials can require rejection. If accepted, the shader must obtain a suitable lighting value at the hit; it cannot sample arbitrary tone-mapped final color without changing the transport quantity and risking feedback.

result = trace_hzb(ray, depth_pyramid, thickness_rule)
if result.accepted:
    radiance = sample_valid_screen_lighting(result)
else:
    append_unresolved_ray(origin, direction, distance_interval,
                          receiver_id, sample_id, pdf, trace_flags)

The output record must carry the state later stages depend on. A compacted queue needs the original receiver/sample mapping so the result lands in the right directional cell. A hit bit must be distinct from a zero-radiance result: a black wall and a failed trace are not the same observation. The remaining distance interval and any bias must match how the next tracing stage interprets the origin.

Make traversal and fallback independently testable

Begin with a slow screen-space ray marcher at full-resolution depth. Transform the origin and direction into view space, clip the segment to the valid near plane and screen rectangle, and step through projected pixels. Use perspective-correct interpolation: a straight world-space ray is not a constant-depth linear interpolation in screen coordinates. Compare a conservative ray-depth interval against the represented surface-depth interval. Refine a candidate crossing before accepting it.

Then add the hierarchy. Each coarse HZB texel bounds the depths of its children according to the producer's convention. At a level, advance to the next projected cell boundary when the whole ray interval is safely in front of the represented bound. Descend when a crossing is possible. At the finest level, apply the thickness and hit-validity rules. Limit both the distance and iteration count. Leaving the view, exhausting steps, or finding an ambiguous crossing returns unresolved work, not certified empty space.

Test the optimized result against the slow marcher on a plane, a thin column, a staircase silhouette, and a ray leaving the screen. Then test both against actual world intersections. The first comparison finds traversal bugs; the second measures the representation's missing information.

For world fallback, prefix-scan the unresolved flags into a compact list and preserve the original result address. Suppose rays 0,2,7 are unresolved: queue slots 0,1,2 must still write back to results 0,2,7. Keep hit distance, hit validity, and radiance validity separate. A valid black surface hit is useful geometric evidence even when its radiance is zero. Resetting all three to zero erases the distinction between an unlit wall and missing representation.

Represent world geometry with distance fields

A signed distance field approximates distance to a surface. For a point outside the surface, an exact field reports distance d to the nearest surface, so a ray can safely advance by d without crossing anything. Repeatedly sample and advance until near zero, beyond the maximum distance, or over the iteration budget. This is sphere tracing.

Mesh distance fields preserve local object detail, while a global distance field combines scene objects into clipmapped volumes with coarser coverage farther from the camera. A practical query can use detailed object fields near the origin and global fields for longer distances. Transform positions and distances consistently under object scale. Handle the inside/outside sign, two-sided meshes, thin surfaces, grid interpolation, and clipmap transitions. A missing or severely compressed wall in this representation can leak light even when rasterization shows the wall perfectly.

Implement a distance-field step without mixing coordinate systems

Store the field's local bounds, world-to-local transform, voxel dimensions, distance scale/bias, and validity. Transform a world sample into local coordinates, map it into the volume's texel domain, interpolate the encoded value, then decode its distance units. If the object has uniform world scale s, a local distance d becomes sd. For a nonuniform scale, multiplying a positive local distance by the smallest singular value of the local-to-world linear transform gives a conservative lower bound on world distance. Multiplying by the largest axis scale can step through a surface.

t = minimum_trace_distance
while t < maximum_trace_distance and steps < maximum_steps:
    x = origin + t * direction
    sample = read_valid_distance_field(x)
    if sample is unavailable: return unresolved
    d = decode_distance_in_world_units(sample)
    if d <= surface_tolerance: return candidate_hit(t)
    step = conservative_step(d, field_error_bound, footprint)
    if step cannot make reliable progress: return unresolved
    t += step
return miss_if_coverage_and_distance_are_complete_else_unresolved

An exact signed distance outside a closed surface supplies a safe step. A filtered, quantized voxel field is approximate: if a proven absolute error bound is ε, max(d−ε,0) is a conservative positive distance bound. Production fields instead use particular expansion, step-factor, minimum-step and tolerance rules. A minimum step can deliberately sacrifice conservatism to escape slow progress, but it is not an exact geometric guarantee. A two-sided field for a sheet also does not supply the same inside/outside meaning as a closed solid.

Build the first version with one object, then compare it against triangle intersections for rays near edges and under scaled transforms. Add object bounds to avoid sampling unrelated volumes. A global union field can use the minimum distance of contributing objects, but a finite clipmap covers only a bounded region. Exiting its coverage requires another representation or an explicit unresolved result, not a declaration that the entire world is empty.

A global-field hit supplies a location and an estimated normal; it does not inherently identify a triangle or material. The inspected Lumen path therefore consults a separate page object grid. It offsets the lookup position using the field normal, resolves the global-field page, decodes candidate GPU Scene instance indices, maps each instance to a mesh-card set, and accumulates compatible card samples. The page object grid is a separate data structure from the distance texture. A field can show an occluding wall while its lighting lookup has no valid mesh-card identity. See the field-hit to card-lookup bridge. The Surface Cache chapter follows the remaining address translation.

In Sponza, a column, a cloth sheet, and a thick masonry wall cannot be expected to survive a coarse field equally well. The mesh must be split and built so that useful cards and distance fields exist for its geometry. Importing the whole atrium as one enormous mesh can give a recognizable camera image while making secondary tracing and surface coverage poor.

Hardware tracing improves intersections, while shading stays a separate choice

Hardware ray tracing traverses acceleration structures built from supported geometry. Its hit can be more faithful than a distance-field hit, especially for detailed meshes, but the result still needs shading. One mode shades the hit through the Surface Cache. Another evaluates more of the hit material and direct lighting. The first can combine exact triangle visibility with approximate diffuse cached radiance. Enabling hardware ray tracing therefore does not by itself make the renderer a path tracer.

Geometry representation choices remain: Nanite fallback geometry versus supported native tracing, skinned updates, masked opacity, instance transforms, material hit groups, and tracing bounds. Keep intersection quality and shading quality as two independent switches in experiments.

For world-tracing continuation, compaction gives the GPU a dense list of selected unresolved rays. A prefix sum or atomic allocator assigns output slots; indirect dispatch dimensions then depend on the remaining count. This avoids repeating the world trace for already-resolved rays. Other queues use other predicates: the reflection chapter also compacts hits that still need lighting. A correct implementation also handles empty dispatches, maximum queue capacity, overflow policy, resource transitions, and synchronization. Asynchronous compute can overlap independent tasks; it cannot let a consumer read an unfinished producer.
